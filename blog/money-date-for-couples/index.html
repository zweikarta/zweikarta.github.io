Most couples don't avoid talking about money because they don't care. They avoid it because the conversation has no shape. It starts with a bill or a surprise purchase, someone feels judged, and it's over before either of you has said what you actually meant.

A money date gives that conversation a shape. It's a short, planned talk about your shared money, on a day you both agreed on, with a clear beginning and a clear end. No ambush, no spreadsheet marathon.

We're Zweikarta, a two-person app studio in Berlin, and we're a couple too. We built GeldSpace, a wealth tracker for couples, around this exact monthly ritual. Here's how we think about it, plus the questions we wrote for it.

What is a money date?

A money date is a regular check-in where two partners look at their money together and talk about it. It isn't a budget meeting. Nobody is there to audit the other person's coffee habit.

A good one has three parts:

  1. A quick look at where you stand. What you own together, what you owe, and what changed since last time.
  2. A conversation. A few questions about how that feels and what you want next.
  3. A clear end. You stop on time, even if there's more to say. There's always next month.

Ten to twenty minutes is plenty.

Why once a month?

Weekly is too often for most couples. Not much changes in a week, and the talk quickly turns into a chore. Once a year is too rare: by the time you sit down there are twelve months to unpack, and it gets heavy.

Monthly sits in the middle. Salaries, rent and loan payments already run on a monthly rhythm, so there's usually something real to talk about, but never so much that it's overwhelming.

Pick a fixed day, like the first of the month or the Sunday after payday, and put it in both calendars. A fixed day matters more than the perfect day. When it's already planned, nobody has to be the one who says "we need to talk about money".

Before you sit down: get your numbers current

The quickest way to derail a money date is to spend it hunting for numbers. "What's in the savings account again?" "Did the loan go down?" Ten minutes later you're both on your phones, in different banking apps.

So do the numbers first, separately, before the date. Each of you checks the accounts you look after and notes the current value. Keep it to what you own and what you owe:

  • cash and savings
  • investments, like a stock portfolio or a fund
  • property, if you have any
  • gold or crypto, if you hold some
  • loans and other debts

What you own minus what you owe is your net worth. Looking at it once a month is far more useful than checking it every day: monthly changes are big enough to notice and small enough to talk about calmly.

How to run a 10-minute money date

Here's a simple flow that works.

1. Start with something good. Open with one thing that went well: a savings goal you reached, a loan you finished paying, or simply that you're sitting down to do this at all. A good start sets the tone for everything after it.

2. Look at what moved. Go through the biggest changes since last month. Not every number, just the one or two that changed most. If something went down, say what happened before anyone has to ask.

3. Talk about one thing. Pick a single topic and really talk about it. One good conversation beats five rushed ones.

4. Look ahead. Is a bigger expense coming up? Is there anything you want to plan for, or stop worrying about?

5. Stop on time. End when you said you would. Leftover topics go on next month's list.

And make it nice. Coffee at your favourite café, a glass of wine on the sofa, a walk around the block. It's called a date for a reason.

20 money date questions for couples

These are the questions we wrote for GeldSpace. Pick three per date: one to look back, one about what changed or how it feels, and one to look ahead.

Looking back

  • What's one thing in our shared money you're proud of this year? Big or small, a first deposit counts.
  • Which money decision from the last few months are you glad we made together?
  • When did money feel easy between us recently?
  • Which moment this year made you feel like a team with money?
  • What did you learn about me from how I handle money?

What changed

  • Our savings grew this month. What helped, and how does it feel?
  • Our savings dipped this month. What was it for, and does that feel okay to both of us?
  • We owe a little less on the loan. How does it feel to see it go down?
  • Something new is in our shared money. What made us decide to share it?

How it feels

  • How do you feel about money this month, in one word each?
  • Was there a money moment this month that felt stressful? What helped?
  • What makes you feel safe when you think about our money?
  • Is there anything about money you've wanted to say but haven't yet?
  • How did your family talk about money when you were growing up?
  • What's one thing I do with money that you appreciate?

Looking ahead

  • Is a bigger expense coming up that we should plan for together?
  • What's one thing you'd like our shared money to make possible next year?
  • Is there something you'd like to stop worrying about? What would help?
  • What does "enough" look like for the two of us?
  • Is there anything you'd like to move into our shared money, or keep separate?

One rule for the "how it feels" questions: listen first. There are no wrong feelings, and you don't have to solve anything in ten minutes.

You don't have to share everything

A lot of money advice for couples assumes you either merge everything or keep it all separate. Most couples live somewhere in between. There's a joint account for rent, a shared savings pot, maybe a home you own together. And there's money each of you keeps for yourself.

That's completely fine. A money date works best when it's about what you've chosen to share. Nobody should feel they have to open every account to have a good conversation.

Four things that turn a money date into a fight

  • Surprises. Don't use the money date to reveal a big purchase. Mention big things when they happen, and use the date to talk about them calmly.
  • Line-by-line spending reviews. Going through every transaction feels like an audit. Look at the bigger picture instead: what you own, what you owe, and how it changed.
  • Keeping score. "I put in more this month" helps nobody. If contributions feel unfair, talk about the feeling, not the tally.
  • Going on too long. An hour-long money talk is exhausting. Short and regular beats long and rare.

Common questions

How often should couples have a money date?

Once a month works for most couples. It matches how salaries and bills work, and it's often enough that nothing piles up.

How long should a money date take?

Ten to twenty minutes, if your numbers are ready before you start. If it keeps running longer, you're probably trying to cover too much at once.

Do we need joint finances to have a money date?

No. You can keep your money completely separate and still have a money date about the things you share, like rent, a holiday or a loan.

Is a money date the same as budgeting?

No. Budgeting looks at spending, line by line. A money date looks at the bigger picture: what you own together, what you owe, and how you both feel about it.
