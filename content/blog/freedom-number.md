::: answer
Your freedom number is roughly how much your family would need invested so that it could cover your yearly spending on its own. A common rule of thumb is 25 times what you spend in a year. If pensions will pay part of your spending later, you need less. It's a rough target to aim at, not a promise.
:::

::: takeaway
- Freedom number ≈ 25 × yearly spending, based on the "4% rule".
- Pensions you'll receive reduce it; years before they start add to it.
- Start from your spending, not your income. And treat the result as a rough guide.
:::

"How much is enough?" is one of the hardest money questions a couple can ask, and most never put a number on it. Without a number, it's hard to tell whether you're doing well, falling behind, or already further along than you think.

## What is a freedom number?

It's the amount that would let your investments pay for how you live, so that working becomes a choice. You may know it as a FIRE number, a retirement number or simply "enough".

It starts from one figure: what your family spends in a year.

## Where does "25 times your spending" come from?

From research on how much people can take out of their investments each year without running out. In 1994, financial planner William Bengen tested US market history and found that taking out 4% in the first year, then adjusting for inflation, lasted at least 30 years in every period he looked at. The 1998 [Trinity study](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_study) found a similar result: with half in stocks and half in bonds, a 4% start lasted 30 years in 95% of historical periods.

If you can take out 4% a year, you need 1 ÷ 0.04 = **25 times** your yearly spending.

The rule has limits. It's based on past US markets, which may not repeat. It was built for 30 years, and an early retirement can last longer. Morningstar's [2025 retirement income research](https://www.morningstar.com/lp/the-state-of-retirement-income) puts a safe starting rate at 3.9% for 30 years, and 3.5% for 35 years. So many people use a lower rate for extra room:

| Withdrawal rate | Multiply spending by |
| ---: | ---: |
| 4% | 25 |
| 3.5% | about 29 |
| 3% | about 33 |

## How to work it out, step by step

1. **Find your yearly spending.** Everything you spend in a year, including rent or mortgage payments. Bank statements for the last 12 months give a good estimate. Use what you'd spend once you've stopped working, if you expect it to change.
2. **Multiply by 25.** That's the basic number.
3. **Subtract what pensions will cover.** If state or work pensions will pay part of your spending later, you only need to fund the rest: 25 × (spending − yearly pensions).
4. **Add the gap years.** If you plan to stop before the pensions start, you need to cover the full spending until they do: add (years until pensions start) × (yearly pensions).
5. **Compare with what you have.** Use what you could actually use: savings and investments, not your home. Divide what you have by your number to see how far along you are.

## A worked example

**EXAMPLE:** a made-up couple. The numbers are invented to show the math, and the same steps work in any currency. Example, not a forecast or advice.

- They spend **48,000** a year.
- Basic number: 25 × 48,000 = **1,200,000**.
- They expect pensions of **18,000** a year together from age 67.
- From 67, investments only need to cover 48,000 − 18,000 = 30,000. 25 × 30,000 = **750,000**.
- They'd like to have the option to stop at 60, so they need to fill a 7-year gap: 7 × 18,000 = **126,000**.
- Their rough freedom number: 750,000 + 126,000 = **876,000**.

Today they have 175,000 in savings, investments and pension pots. That's 175,000 ÷ 876,000, about **20%** of the way.

Notice what moved the number the most: spending. If they spent 4,000 less a year, from 67 they'd need 25 × 26,000 = 650,000 instead of 750,000, which takes 100,000 off the target. That's why spending, not income, is where the number starts.

## What's different for a family or a couple

- **Two retirement ages.** Partners are often a few years apart, and their pensions start at different times. Count the gap years from when you want to stop until each pension starts, one by one.
- **Children change your spending, then change it back.** Spending usually rises while children live at home and falls after. Base the number on what you expect to spend once you'd use it.
- **Two incomes, one number.** You don't need to split the number between you. It's a household target. Who saves what toward it can stay in each person's own money.
- **Private money.** If you keep some money separate, decide together whether it counts toward the shared number. Either is fine; just be clear.

## Common mistakes

- **Starting from income.** You need to replace what you spend, not what you earn.
- **Counting your home.** You can't live off a home you live in. Leave it out unless you plan to sell or downsize. Our post on [whether to count your home in your net worth](/blog/home-in-net-worth/) explains why.
- **Forgetting taxes.** Withdrawals from some accounts are taxed. If yours will be, add a margin.
- **Treating it as exact.** It's a rough target. Update it once a year, for example at your [annual money meeting](/blog/annual-money-meeting/).

::: faq
### Is the 4% rule still valid?
It's a useful starting point, not a guarantee. It's based on past US returns over 30 years. Recent research suggests a slightly lower rate, around 3.5% to 4%, especially for longer retirements. Many people use 3.5% to be safe.

### Do I need to reach my freedom number to stop working?
No. Many people slow down before they reach it, for example by working part-time while their investments keep growing. That idea is called Coast FIRE.

### What counts toward the number?
Money you could draw on: savings, investments and pension pots. Not your home, unless you plan to sell it.
:::
