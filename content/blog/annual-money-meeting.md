::: answer
An annual money meeting is one hour, once a year, when a couple looks at their money together. You write down where you stand today, compare it with a year ago, talk through what's coming next year, and pick one or two things to try. It isn't a budget review. It's a check of the big picture.
:::

::: takeaway
- One hour, once a year, with four parts: where we stand, what changed, what's coming, what we'll try.
- Bring numbers, not opinions. Rough totals are enough.
- End with no more than two decisions, and put next year's date in the calendar before you get up.
:::

Most couples talk about money only when something forces it: a big bill, a move, a new job. The talk then happens under pressure, about one problem, and the big picture never comes up.

A yearly meeting fixes that. Late December and early January work well, because work is quiet and the new year gives you a natural line to draw.

## Why have a money meeting at all?

Because couples often don't share the same picture. In [Fidelity's 2024 Couples & Money study](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20240201922762/en/Love-Money-Most-Couples-Give-Themselves-High-Marks-in-Communication-Yet-Fidelity-Study-Reveals-Hidden-Frustrations-in-Couples-Financial-Future), which asked both partners in 1,794 US couples the same questions, 53% of couples not yet retired disagreed about how much they need to save for retirement. Most couples in the study still rated their money communication highly.

So the meeting isn't about fixing a bad relationship with money. It's about checking that you both see the same numbers.

## What to prepare (15 minutes, the day before)

- **A list of what you own and owe, with today's balances.** Savings, investments, pensions, your home at a rough value, and loans. If you did this last year, update the same list.
- **Last year's totals,** if you have them.
- **Anything big you know is coming:** a car, a move, a child, a change in work.

Each of you brings your own part. Private money can stay private: bring a total for your own accounts if you prefer not to share the details.

## The agenda: one hour in four parts

| Part | Time | The question |
| :--- | ---: | :--- |
| **1. Where we stand** | 15 min | What do we own and owe today, shared and separately? |
| **2. What changed** | 15 min | What grew, what dipped, and why? |
| **3. What's coming** | 15 min | What do we expect next year that will cost or bring money? |
| **4. What we'll try** | 15 min | Which one or two things do we want to do differently? |

### 1. Where we stand

Add up your net worth: what you own minus what you owe. Most couples find it easiest to count a shared number for what you own together, plus a private number each. Our guide on [how to calculate your net worth as a couple](/blog/net-worth-as-a-couple/) walks through it with an example, and the [net worth page](/geldspace/net-worth/) adds it up for you in your browser.

### 2. What changed

Compare with a year ago. Don't judge the number yet; explain it. Did savings grow because you saved more, or because markets rose? Did a loan get smaller? Write one sentence for each big change.

If this is your first meeting, skip this part. Next year you'll have something to compare with.

### 3. What's coming

List the events you already know about. A wedding to attend, a car that's getting old, a parent who may need help, a planned break from work. You don't need exact amounts. "Around a month's spending" is useful enough to plan around.

### 4. What we'll try

Pick one or two things, not ten. For example:

- Raise the monthly amount you put aside by a small step.
- Pay off one small loan.
- Start counting your net worth once a month instead of once a year.
- Each keep a set amount a month to spend without asking.

Write them down with a name next to each one.

## Questions that help the talk

- What are we glad about from this year, money-wise?
- What worried either of us, even a little?
- If we had an extra month's spending next year, what would we do with it?
- What does "enough" look like for us, roughly?
- Is there anything one of us would like more say in?

The last two often lead to the best conversations. "Enough" in particular is worth coming back to every year; it's the idea behind a freedom number.

## How to keep it calm

- **Set a time and stick to it.** One hour, then stop, even if you're not done.
- **Choose a good place.** A café or a walk works better than the kitchen table where the bills pile up.
- **Talk about numbers, not each other.** "Our savings dipped" goes down better than "you spent too much".
- **Leave blame out of last year.** You can't change it; you can only explain it.

## After the meeting

Put next year's date in both your calendars before you get up. Then keep the habit small during the year: a few minutes once a month to update your numbers, so next year's first part takes five minutes instead of fifteen. Our [three-question monthly money talk](/blog/how-to-talk-about-money-as-a-couple/) is a good fit.

::: faq
### How long should an annual money meeting take?
About an hour. Shorter and you skip the "what's coming" part; much longer and it starts to feel like an exam. If something big needs more time, give it its own meeting.

### What if one of us doesn't want to do it?
Start smaller. Ask for 20 minutes and only the first part, where you stand. Many people resist because they expect to be judged. A short, numbers-only first meeting shows them they won't be.

### Should we share every account?
No. Shared money should be visible to both of you. For private money, a total each, or nothing at all, is fine if you both agreed on it.
:::
