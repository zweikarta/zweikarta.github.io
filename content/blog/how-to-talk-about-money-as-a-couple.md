Most couples know they should talk about money. Few ever find a good moment for it. It's late, the week was long, and nobody wants to start something heavy. So the talk waits until a surprise bill or a big decision forces it, and then it happens at the worst possible time.

It doesn't have to be that way. Here's why these talks are so hard to start, the ways couples usually handle them, and a simple format that keeps them short and calm.

## Why money talks are hard to start

**They feel too big.** "Our finances" isn't a topic, it's a whole weekend. When a conversation has no edges, nobody wants to be the one who opens it.

**They come with a mood.** If money only comes up when something is wrong, money itself starts to feel like something being wrong. "Can we talk about money?" can sound a lot like "what did you do?"

**One person has to bring it up.** Without a fixed time, the same person usually raises it. That person starts to feel like a nag, and the other starts to feel checked on.

**You're not looking at the same picture.** Each of you has your own apps and your own rough idea of where things stand. So the talk turns into comparing guesses instead of deciding anything.

**There's no finish line.** A good talk needs an end. Without one, it's easy to drift from "how are we doing?" into every money worry you've ever had.

None of this is really about the money. It's about the shape of the conversation, and that part you can change.

## Three ways couples handle money talks

![Three patterns over a year: a few talks at random times, one big sit-down, and a short talk every month.](ways.png "Three common patterns over a year.")

| Cadence | Description | Pros | Watch Out For |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Only When Needed** | Reacting to unexpected bills or changes | Zero advance planning | Turns money into a stress trigger |
| **Annual Sit-Down** | Exhaustive deep-dive once or twice a year | Very thorough | Hard to schedule, numbers go stale fast |
| **Monthly Money Date** | 10–15 minute calm check-in once a month | Small issues caught early, stays low stress | Needs a fixed recurring day |

### 1. Only when something comes up

You talk when there's a reason: a repair, a job change, a big purchase. It takes no planning and feels natural. The downside is that every talk starts with a problem or a deadline, so money slowly becomes a stressful subject.

### 2. One big sit-down

Once or twice a year, you sit down and go through everything. It's thorough, and it can feel great when it's done. But it's a lot of work, so it's easy to put off, and the numbers are out of date a few months later.

### 3. A little, every month

You keep a short talk on a fixed day, often called a money date. Nothing is decided under pressure, and small changes get noticed while they're still small. It only works if it stays easy enough to keep doing.

There's no right answer here, and most couples will still need the odd bigger talk. But a short monthly habit means those bigger talks don't have to start from zero.

## A simple format: three questions

A money date doesn't need an agenda. Three questions are enough, in this order.

1. **What felt good about money this month?** Start with something positive, even a small one: a loan that got a bit smaller, a month with no surprises, a trip you enjoyed. It sets the mood and shows you this isn't an audit.
2. **Is there anything to move into "ours", or back to "mine"?** Things change. A savings pot one of you started might become something you're saving for together. If you're exploring how to divide shared vs personal money, see [our guide on how to manage money as a couple without merging everything](/blog/manage-money-as-a-couple/). "No, nothing" is a perfectly good answer.
3. **What are we looking forward to?** A holiday, a new sofa, a plan that's still half an idea. This turns a talk about numbers into a talk about the two of you, and it gives you a finish line.

On a quiet month, this can be over in a couple of minutes. The first few will take longer, because you'll have more to say. That's fine.

## Habits that keep it calm

**Pick a fixed day.** The first of the month, the first Sunday, whatever is easy to remember. With a fixed day, nobody has to bring it up.

**Look at the same numbers.** A rough, shared picture is enough. Round numbers are fine, as long as you're both looking at the same ones.

**Don't solve anything big at the table.** If something large comes up, name it and give it its own time. "Let's talk about the car on Saturday" is a complete answer.

**Talk about what changed, not who did it.** The moment a sentence starts with "why did you", it stops being a date. If something dipped this month, it dipped. Say so and move on to what's next.

**You don't have to show every number.** A money date works fine when each of you keeps some things private. You only need to talk about the part you share.

You can start all of this with a coffee and no tools at all. Next time you're both at the table, ask the first question and see where it goes. Then put a date in the calendar for next month.

::: faq
### What should we do if one partner avoids talking about money?
Start tiny. Avoid bringing up past spending, budgets, or debts. Instead, set a low-stakes 10-minute timer and ask just one question: "What felt good about money this month?" Building a positive, calm association first makes future conversations much easier.

### Should we talk about money over dinner or at home?
Pair the conversation with something pleasant — a morning coffee on a relaxed weekend, a walk, or a quiet drink. Avoid talking late in the evening before bed or immediately after arriving home exhausted from work.

### Do we need a detailed spreadsheet for a monthly money date?
No. In fact, spreadsheets often derail conversations into line-item auditing ("Why did you spend €40 here?"). Focusing on high-level net worth (total assets minus liabilities) keeps the focus on shared growth rather than daily nitpicking.
:::
